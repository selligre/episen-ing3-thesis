\section{Pipeline Implementation}
\label{sec:three:one}

Motivated by the architectural limitations of kernel-level access established in Chapter 2, the pipeline developed for this thesis relocates the detection effort entirely to the server side, treating the stream of gameplay telemetry a match server already produces as the sole evidentiary basis for suspicion. The system is implemented in Python and organised around a linear execution flow: telemetry ingestion, rule-based scoring, sequential scoring, persistence, benchmarking, and reporting. The code is publicly available at \url{https://github.com/selligre/episen-ing3-thesis-ac-server}.

\subsection{Ingestion and Scoring Objectives}
\label{sec:three:one:one}

The pipeline pursues two complementary objectives rather than a single detection outcome. The first is to automate the transformation of raw telemetry-derived features into a continuous suspicion score for every player, replacing the manual, case-by-case inspection a moderator would otherwise perform. The second, and arguably the more crucial objective given the false-positive risk, is to structure this score into a prioritisation mechanism for human review rather than an autonomous sanctioning decision. This design deliberately echoes Valve's own Overwatch tribunal, in which trusted community reviewers audit cases pre-selected by an automated system rather than acting on raw statistical output alone; the suspicion score produced here is intended to feed an equivalent triage queue, surfacing the accounts most likely to warrant scrutiny while leaving the final determination to an experienced human reviewer who can recognise contextual nuance the algorithm cannot. The system is tuned to avoid false accusations at the cost of missing some cheaters outright at the automated threshold. The underlying premise is that a prioritisation tool which never wrongly flags a legitimate player is more deployable, and more defensible under the proportionality requirements discussed in Appendix D.3, than one which maximises raw detection at the expense of player trust.

Because parsing official match demos to obtain real telemetry would require infrastructure and engineering time far beyond the scope of this thesis, ingestion is performed on synthetically generated player telemetry rather than data extracted from actual CS2 matches, a constraint discussed further in Section 3.2.3 and in the conclusion as a limitation.

\subsection{Algorithmic Architecture and Core Concepts}
\label{sec:three:one:two}

The heuristic layer evaluates each player session against eight weighted flags, each targeting one of the mechanical signatures of automation or information cheats described in Chapter 1: an abnormally low time-to-damage, a pre-aim delta indicating the crosshair was already resting on the enemy's position before engagement, an extreme headshot rate, a near-perfect spray-control deviation, an inhumanly short reaction time, a combined kill/death ratio and damage-per-round outlier, an implausibly high clutch win rate, and a consistency bonus described below. Rather than firing as a binary trigger, each flag returns a continuous intensity between the threshold value and a calibrated "cheater-centre" value derived from CS2 community benchmarks and the feature distributions reported by \citeauthor{Loo2025} \cite{Loo2025}; a time-to-damage flag, for instance, activates below 200 ms and reaches full intensity around 40 ms, while a pre-aim flag activates below 12° and saturates near 1.5°. This continuous formulation is intentional: it allows an exceptional but legitimate performance to partially activate a flag without immediately being treated as equivalent to a clear cheater signature, deferring the separation to the aggregation stage rather than to any single threshold crossing.

At the player level, a flag only contributes to the score if it fires in at least 30\% of that player's sessions, weighted by its average intensity across the sessions in which it fired; this cross-session requirement is what prevents a single exceptional match from inflating a player's score. A further consistency bonus is awarded when at least four distinct flags each fire in 80\% or more of a player's sessions, explicitly rewarding persistence over isolated outliers. This mechanism helps reducing the high-skill-bias mitigation: an individual flag such as an extreme headshot rate can plausibly describe a strong legitimate player having a good run, but the joint and sustained co-occurrence of several such flags across a large share of a player's match history is harder to attribute to skill alone. The obtained raw score is normalised to a 0–100 scale.

The sequential component is a single-layer Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network that consumes, for each session, the round-by-round sequence of five normalised features (mean time-to-damage, pre-aim delta, spray deviation, reaction time, and headshot rate) and outputs a cheat probability via a dense sigmoid head. This network is not trained on labelled data and is instead initialised with weights that manually encode the expected directional relationship between each feature and cheat probability, established from the same literature and profile calibration referenced above. This choice is made explicit: the LSTM is presented as a proof of architectural soundness, demonstrating that the sequential, round-by-round structure of gameplay data is the appropriate representation for capturing behavioural persistence over time, without overstating what an untrained model can be credited with detecting. Session-level probabilities are aggregated per player using the 75th percentile rather than the mean, so that a player who cheats intermittently rather than in every session is still surfaced. The final suspicion score combines both layers as a weighted sum:
\begin{equation}
    \text{suspicion\_score} = 0.6 \times \text{heuristic\_score} + 0.4 \times \left(100 \times \text{lstm\_prob}\right)
    \label{eq:suspicion_score}
\end{equation}
giving the interpretable, empirically calibrated heuristic layer the dominant weight while allowing the sequential component to act as a directional regulariser.

\subsection{Practical System Deployment}
\label{sec:three:one:three}

The pipeline executes as a single linear sequence of seven stages: synthetic telemetry generation for three hundred players evenly split across the casual, skilled, and cheater profiles; heuristic scoring; LSTM scoring and score combination; persistence of the scored player set to structured JSON, including a dedicated memory file simulating the cross-match history a deployed system would accumulate for each account; benchmark computation against the ground-truth profile labels; console reporting; and generation of readable outputs. This batch-processing pipeline model was chosen deliberately over a real-time, per-event architecture: because the scoring logic operates on session-level and cross-session aggregates rather than individual ticks, asynchronous post-match processing is sufficient to produce a usable suspicion score without the engineering overhead of a live telemetry stream, though this trade-off, batch versus real-time, is inherent to the design choice made here rather than a limitation this thesis further develops. For future work extending this pipeline to real match data, the awpy library (an open-source CS2 demo parser) is identified as a natural ingestion point, replacing the synthetic generator without requiring changes to the scoring layers downstream.

\section{Dataset and Features}
\label{sec:three:two}

\subsection{Telemetry Data Model}
\label{sec:three:two:one}

Consistent with the mechanical focus of the heuristic and sequential scoring layers, the telemetry data model implemented in this thesis is deliberately restricted to the aim-related and tactical signatures directly exploitable by those layers, rather than attempting to model the full range of features a production system might eventually ingest. Nine per-session features are retained: mean time-to-damage, mean pre-aim delta, mean spray deviation, mean reaction time, and headshot rate, which together constitute the aim-vector group directly targeted by five of the eight heuristic flags described in the architecture; and average utility damage, clutch win rate, kill/death ratio, and average damage per round, which capture broader performance consistency without being tied to a single mechanical action. This narrower scope reflects a conscious trade-off: it keeps every feature traceable to a specific, previously documented cheat behaviour rather than diluting the model with signals whose relationship to cheating is indirect or contextual.

Spatial and social features (cross-match positioning consistency, opening-duel and trading-efficiency windows, or premade-lobby dependencies) were considered during the design phase but are not part of the implemented telemetry model, and are treated here as a natural direction for extending the pipeline rather than as a current limitation of the mechanical layer itself; this distinction, and the reasoning behind it, is developed further in Section 3.4.2.

Each recorded kill event additionally carries a hit-zone label (head, chest, stomach, or leg), sampled per profile according to the weighted distribution. This choice directly mirrors the zone-dependent damage model described in Appendix A.2, where aim assistance derives much of its lethal advantage not merely from guaranteeing a hit, but from consistently redirecting that hit toward the highest-multiplier zone; encoding this bias explicitly in the synthetic data allows the headshot-rate feature to carry the same discriminative weight it would in an authentic dataset.

\subsection{Data Sources and Profiling}
\label{sec:three:two:two}

In the absence of parsed match demos, telemetry is produced by a synthetic generator that samples each of the nine features above from a profile-specific Normal distribution, independently calibrated for three player populations (casual, skilled, and cheater) and clamped to physically plausible bounds after sampling. The calibration itself draws on three sources: publicly available CS2 community benchmarks for reaction time and aim-consistency metrics, the feature-level analysis of confirmed cheating behaviour reported by \citeauthor{Loo2025} in their transformer-based detection study \cite{Loo2025}, and the cheat behaviour taxonomy established by \citeauthor{Collins2024} \cite{Collins2024}, which informs how the widening or narrowing of a distribution's variance across profiles is meant to approximate genuine population heterogeneity rather than three artificially uniform archetypes.

The resulting profiles are structured to overlap deliberately at their extremities rather than forming three cleanly separated clusters. The skilled distribution for time-to-damage, for example, is centred at 250 ms with a standard deviation of 60 ms, while the cheater distribution is centred at 160 ms with a standard deviation of 45 ms; the two ranges intersect meaningfully around 200 ms, so that a proportion of skilled players will, on some sessions, produce a reaction speed indistinguishable from a cheater's typical performance. This overlap is the central concept behind the design of the dataset: it is what allows the pipeline's separation strategy (joint, cross-session consistency) to be meaningfully tested at all. This feature-level overlap is substantially resolved by the aggregation stage itself, which is the mechanism the dataset is designed to put under stress. The same logic governs the hit-zone weighting: cheater profiles are assigned an 82\% probability of landing a headshot against 45\% for skilled and 20\% for casual players, again chosen to be markedly higher without being categorically disjoint from legitimate high-level play.

At the population level, the simulation generates one hundred players per profile, each with twenty independent match sessions of fifteen rounds, and an average of 1.2 Poisson-sampled kills per round (yielding a dataset large enough to support the per-flag fire-rate and cross-session consistency) while remaining computationally light enough to regenerate and re-tune during the iterative calibration process.

\subsection{Data Constraints and Limitations}
\label{sec:three:two:three}

The most consequential limitation of the dataset is structural rather than statistical: it is entirely synthetic, and no volume of careful distribution tuning substitutes for behavioural regularities that only emerge from real matches: momentary hesitation before a risky peek, fatigue-driven degradation over a long session, or the correlated adjustments a genuine player makes across consecutive rounds. The Normal-distribution sampling approach used here treats each feature as independently drawn within a session, which cannot reproduce the finer inter-feature correlations a real player's telemetry would exhibit, nor the gradual behavioural drift a genuine cheater might display when adjusting their tool's aggressiveness to evade detection. Server-side constraints tied to tick rate and snapshot granularity, which bound what any telemetry-based system can observe in principle, are discussed separately in Appendix C.2 and are not reproduced here, since they apply identically to a hypothetical real-data deployment and are not specific to the synthetic dataset itself.

A second limitation concerns the absence of account-level and social signals that an experienced human reviewer would routinely factor into a manual judgement: account age relative to displayed skill level, hours played, or the presence of previously banned accounts within a player's social graph. These are exactly the kind of contextual cues a moderator "learns to recognise" but that the current feature set does not encode at all, since the telemetry model is restricted, by design, to per-match mechanical and performance signals. Their absence does not undermine the results, which concern the separability of mechanical signatures specifically, but it does mean the suspicion score should be read as one input among several a complete review workflow would need, rather than a self-sufficient verdict: a point returned to when this system is positioned within a broader detection architecture in Section 3.4.2 and the conclusion.

\section{Benchmark Execution and Raw Outputs}
\label{sec:three:three}

\subsection{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:three:three:one}

The benchmark is executed as a single, deterministic pass over the full synthetic population (three hundred players, one hundred per profile, each carrying twenty independent match sessions) generated with a fixed random seed to keep the reported figures reproducible across pipeline runs. Within this controlled environment, the classical heuristic scorer and the sequential LSTM layer are applied sequentially rather than jointly trained: every player first receives a heuristic score from the flag-based procedure, after which the LSTM produces a per-session cheat probability that is itself blended with the normalised heuristic score before being combined into the final suspicion score, following the weighting formula given in Equation~\eqref{eq:suspicion_score}. This sequencing matters, since it means the ranking metric is not evaluating an independently trained classifier, but the discriminative power of the heuristic signal after it has propagated through the calibrated LSTM stage.

Two classification tasks are evaluated from the same set of suspicion scores. The first is a strict binary task (cheater versus non-cheater) obtained by applying the single suspicion threshold of 48 established during calibration; precision, recall, and F1 are computed against this cut. The second is a three-class task that additionally separates casual from skilled players below the cheater threshold, using a secondary cut at a suspicion score of 12; this three-class assignment is what populates the confusion matrix discussed below. The area under the ROC curve is computed separately from either hard cut, directly against the continuous, calibrated suspicion probability, and therefore measures the pipeline's ability to rank cheaters above legitimate players in general, independently of where the operational threshold happens to sit: a distinction that becomes central to the interpretation offered in the analysis.

\subsection{Raw Evaluation Results}
\label{sec:three:three:two}

At the population level, the three profiles separate into three visibly distinct suspicion-score bands, illustrated in Figure 13. Casual players form an extremely tight cluster around a mean of 9.02 (dispersion = 0.05, range 8.88–9.11), reflecting the near-total absence of any heuristic flag activity in that profile. Skilled players sit noticeably higher, with a mean of 11.47 and a wider spread (dispersion = 1.47, range 9.18–16.69) that reflects the occasional, isolated flag activation described below, but remain far below the suspicion threshold in every case. Cheater players occupy a substantially elevated and considerably more dispersed band, with a mean of 46.14 and a standard deviation of 7.24; The median of this distribution (48.12) sits almost exactly on the classification threshold, meaning that roughly half of the cheater population falls on either side of the cut by construction rather than by classification error.

The resulting three-class confusion matrix, presented in Figure 15, shows the casual class perfectly recovered on its own terms (100\% of casual players correctly classified, n = 100, and no casual player ever misclassified as skilled or cheater), while the classification error is distributed across two distinct boundaries. The larger share, by volume, sits at the casual/skilled boundary: 75\% of skilled players are classified as casual, with only the remaining 25\% correctly identified as skilled. The second, and probably the more consequential, sits at the skilled/cheater boundary: 51\% of cheater players are correctly flagged as cheaters, while the remaining 49\% fall instead into the skilled bucket. More importantly, no cheater is ever misclassified as casual, and no casual or skilled player is ever misclassified as a cheater at the operational threshold. The result is confirmed directly by the classification metrics: precision reaches 1.0, meaning zero false positives across the entire three-hundred-player population, while recall stands at 0.51 and F1 at 0.6755. The area under the ROC curve, computed on the continuous suspicion probability independently of the threshold, reaches 1.0: a perfect score indicating that, across the whole population, every cheater ranks strictly above every non-cheater, even when a substantial share of cheaters falls under the hard cut used for binary classification. The interpretation of this apparent tension between a modest recall and a perfect AUC, and its implications for how the system should be deployed, is developed in the analysis of the resulting data.

\section{Analysis and Performance Discussion}
\label{sec:three:four}

\subsection{Detection Results and the High-Skill Bias}
\label{sec:three:four:one}

Read together, the results reported so far show a system that behaves rather safely: no legitimate player, whether casual or skilled, is ever pushed into the cheater class, and no cheater is ever mistaken for a casual one. We can observe that the system's two sources of error, instead of correcting each other, pull in the same direction: toward under-flagging rather than over-flagging. The largest error, the 75\% of skilled players classified as casual, reflects a genuinely conservative separation between ordinary and elevated legitimate play rather than any confusion with cheating behaviour. The smaller but more consequential error, the 49\% of cheaters classified as skilled, is the one that directly motivates the high-skill-bias discussion below. This asymmetry follows directly from the flag design, since casual telemetry never approaches any flag's activation threshold, whereas skilled and cheater profiles were deliberately constructed to overlap at their extremities. This overlap was introduced precisely so that the separation strategy would be tested against a realistic edge case rather than a trivially easy one. The perfect AUC-ROC of 1.0 obtained here should accordingly be read with that caveat attached: it reflects the discriminative power of the pipeline over a synthetic population whose feature distributions are, by construction, cleaner and better behaved than an authentic dataset would be, and it is not directly comparable to the 0.93 AUC reported by \citeauthor{Loo2025} \cite{Loo2025} over 795 real, labelled CS2 matches. Because of the design of its dataset, this thesis was not able to reproduce the genuine gameplay noise.

The flag-level breakdown in Figure 14 makes the mechanism of the high-skill bias directly visible rather than merely asserting it. HS\_RATE\_EXTREME fires for 83\% of skilled sessions against 100\% of cheater sessions: a gap far too narrow for the flag to serve as a standalone signal, since a strong legitimate player sustaining an elevated headshot rate over several sessions is, on its own, statistically almost indistinguishable from a cheater by this measure alone. Every other flag, by contrast, remains largely or entirely silent on skilled players: REACTION\_INHUMAN, KD\_ADR\_COMBO, and critically CONSISTENCY\_BONUS never activate once across the entire skilled population. This last point is the operative one: because CONSISTENCY\_BONUS specifically requires at least four distinct flags to each fire in 80\% or more of a player's sessions, it cannot be triggered by a single strong skill event, however elevated; it is only satisfied when several independent mechanical anomalies co-occur and persist, which is exactly what the cheater profile was calibrated to produce and the skilled profile was not. The high-skill bias is therefore not eliminated by making any individual flag stricter, doing so would only push the threshold further from genuinely exceptional but legitimate play without addressing the underlying ambiguity but by shifting the decisive signal from single-flag intensity to cross-flag, cross-session consistency.

This design choice explains the apparent tension between a recall of 0.51 and a precision of 1.0. The suspicion threshold of 48 was calibrated deliberately close to the cheater distribution's median of 48.12, which guarantees, given the shape of that distribution, that roughly half of all cheaters will fall under the cut by construction. This is an intentional consequence of prioritising a zero false-positive rate over exhaustive detection, in line with the proportionality argument developed in Appendix D.3 and the objective of building a triage tool rather than an autonomous sanctioning mechanism. Considered in isolation, a 51\% recall would appear to be a substantial shortfall. Considered alongside a perfect AUC, however, it indicates something more specific about where this recall shortfall actually sits. Despite the deliberate overlap introduced at the feature level, the aggregation mechanism (cross-session flag persistence and the consistency bonus) collapses that overlap almost entirely by the time scores reach the population level: the skilled distribution (mean 11.47, max 16.69) and the cheater distribution (mean 46.14) no longer intersect at all in Figure 13. The 49\% of cheaters missed at the operational threshold are therefore never confused with skilled players; they fall below the cut within their own, clearly separated distribution, whose median (48.12) sits almost exactly on the threshold by construction. In other words, the imperfect result reflects a conservative choice of cut-off point, not an ambiguity between skilled and cheater profiles. The ambiguity visible at the individual-flag level is what the aggregation stage is designed to resolve, and the population-level separation confirms that it does. A review workflow that processes players in descending order of suspicion score, rather than applying a single binary cut, would therefore still surface every cheater in the dataset ahead of any legitimate player. The fixed threshold determines only how many of them are flagged automatically without human involvement, not how many are, in principle, detectable at all. This distinction between what the score can rank and what a chosen cut-off acts upon becomes the starting point for the optimisation proposed in the following section.

\subsection{Future Optimizations}
\label{sec:three:four:two}
The most direct extension suggested by the analysis above is architectural rather than statistical: replacing the fixed threshold evaluated with a ranked review queue that processes the full population in descending suspicion-score order, up to whatever volume of manual review capacity a given deployment can sustain. Because the AUC-ROC result establishes that cheaters are correctly ranked above legitimate players across the entire score range, such a queue would recover the cheaters currently missed at the 51\%-recall cut without lowering the threshold itself and without reintroducing the false-positive risk that a lower cut would carry. A direct operationalisation of the Overwatch-style triage discussed earlier would requires no change to the underlying scoring logic.

A second direction concerns the LSTM component itself. As established in Section 3.1.2, its weights were manually initialised from calibration literature rather than learned from data, which means its contribution to the final suspicion score currently functions as an informed regulariser rather than an independently trained classifier. Training this layer on genuine round-by-round sequences would allow the sequential component to learn temporal patterns the heuristic layer cannot express by design, such as gradual behavioural drift within a session or the specific timing at which a cheat is toggled on and off, rather than relying solely on hand-calibrated directional priors.

A third direction follows from the decision made in the conception of the pipeline to restrict the telemetry model to mechanical and performance features. Extending it to the spatial and social dimensions set aside there (cross-match positioning consistency, opening-duel and trading-efficiency windows, and premade-lobby dependencies) would not replace the current aim-centric flags but complement them, since these signals are largely orthogonal to raw mechanical execution and could plausibly narrow the skilled/cheater overlap identified in results from a second, independent angle rather than by further tightening thresholds on the same feature family.

Finally, the calibration process itself remains a manual, iterative one: flag thresholds and profile distributions were tuned by hand against community benchmarks and the literature cited in Section 3.2.2, a process that proved workable at the scale of this thesis but would not scale efficiently to a larger feature set or to periodic recalibration against a live player population whose skill distribution shifts over time. Automating this calibration (for instance by refitting profile distributions and flag thresholds against newly confirmed cheater cases as they accumulate) is left as the natural operational counterpart to the detection architecture developed in this chapter.

\section{Comparison with Kernel-level approach}
\label{sec:three:five}

The DMA blind spot already noted above is not the exception in this comparison but its clearest instance of a broader pattern: kernel-level and server-side detection fail on structurally opposite classes of cheat, which makes them complementary rather than substitutable. Kernel-level anti-cheat, as established in Chapter 2, is well-suited to detecting evasion techniques that leave an artefact within the host operating system (an unsigned BYOVD driver, a DKOM-manipulated process list, an injected DLL) but is categorically blind to any vector that never touches host software, DMA cheating being the clearest case. The server-side pipeline developed in this chapter is blind in the opposite direction: because its flags depend on cross-session consistency by design, it has no mechanism for identifying a cheat used briefly, once, and never again, regardless of how blatant that single session might be, since no history exists yet against which to establish persistence. Neither limitation is a flaw specific to their implementation, each follows directly from where its respective trust anchor sits: the host process for the former, the accumulated match record for the latter.

In terms of intrusiveness, the asymmetry is obvious. The experiment conducted in Chapter 2 demonstrated concretely that Ring 0 access reaches indiscriminately into any resident process's memory, game-related or not. The pipeline evaluated here, by contrast, operates exclusively on telemetry the server already generates as the authoritative record of the match itself (Appendix C.1), and at no point requires visibility into any process, file, or memory region outside the game session being scored. This is the basis on which Appendix D.3 argues that a server-side approach more readily satisfies the GDPR's data-minimisation principle: the detection surface is, by construction, restricted to exactly the data the stated purpose requires.

This narrower intrusiveness carries through directly to the adoption dynamics discussed in Appendix D.2. Because no privileged client component is installed, the server-side approach sidesteps both the platform-level exclusion of Linux and Steam Deck users and the always-on monitoring objection that has repeatedly triggered the boycotts and review-bombing episodes catalogued there; the trade-off, in exchange, is the reduced and delayed detection reach just described, rather than any claim that the two dimensions (effectiveness and acceptability) trade off exactly one-for-one.

Taken together, these comparisons support a positioning of the two approaches as distinct layers rather than competitors: kernel-level detection remains the more direct tool against host-resident manipulation, while server-side scoring extends coverage to hardware-assisted and behaviourally anomalous cases the former cannot reach, at the cost of requiring an accumulated match history before a verdict can be reached at all. The triage architecture proposed in Chapter 3 and refined in its "Future Optimisations" section (a ranked review queue feeding human validation rather than an autonomous ban) is what allows this second layer to be deployed without inheriting the intrusiveness of the first, a synthesis developed further in the conclusion.
