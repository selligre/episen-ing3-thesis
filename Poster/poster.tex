% Introduction

\begin{posterbox}[name=problem, span=2, column=0, row=0]{Problem}
	Kernel-level anti-cheats (Ring 0) are becoming the industry standard for stopping cheaters in competitive FPS games such as Counter-Strike 2, but they require granting a driver unrestricted access to the entire system, not just the game. This creates three risks:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Security}: expands the attack surface
		\item \textbf{Privacy}: violates GDPR principles of data minimization
		\item \textbf{Trust}: drives boycotts, excludes Linux users
	\end{itemize}
	This thesis explores an alternative: detecting cheaters through server-side behavioral analysis of gameplay telemetry, without ever touching the player's machine.
\end{posterbox}

% Chapter 2

\begin{posterbox}[name=risks, column=1, below=problem]{Kernel-Level Risks}
    • Kernel-level anti-cheat is stuck in a structural arms race it cannot win: cheat vendors patch around detections in days, while publishers need weeks to safely ship a kernel update \cite{Kuzhii2024}. Worse, it is categorically blind to DMA cheats.

	• To test how far this privileged access actually reaches, Windows-Kernel-Explorer and Cheat Engine were used on an isolated VM to locate and reconstruct a password from an \emph{unrelated} Notepad process's memory. Ring 0 access is not just theoretically broad: it is critically intrusive against any parallel process, like browsers or password managers.
\end{posterbox}

% Chapter 3

\begin{posterbox}[name=flags, span=1, column=0, below=problem]{Detection Flags}
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../images/chap03/flag_frequency.png}
	\end{center}
	Five flags fire in over 90\% of cheater sessions: four stay silent on skilled players, but HS\_RATE also fires on 83\% of skilled sessions, exposing it as the least significant signal on its own. Cross-session persistence across \emph{several} flags is what separates cheaters from skilled players \cite{Loo2025}.
\end{posterbox}

\begin{posterbox}[name=suspicion, span=1, column=2, below=dma]{Suspicion Score}
	Two independent layers combine into one suspicion score per session:
	\begin{center}
		$\text{score} = 0.6 * \text{heuristic} + 0.4 * (100 * \text{lstm})$
	\end{center}
	$heuristic$: 8 threshold-calibrated flags (time-to-damage, pre-aim, reaction time, KD/ADR, clutch, consistency, etc).

	$lstm$: a probability modeling round-by-round behavioral persistence. A flag only counts if it recurs across a player's match history, not a single session.
\end{posterbox}

\begin{posterbox}[name=results, span=2, column=0, below=flags]{Results (synthetic benchmark)}
	\textbf{Zero false positives, 51\% recall:} a conservative threshold flags half of cheaters automatically, the rest rank just below it for review.

	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{../images/chap03/confusion_matrix.png}
	\end{center}

	\textbf{Conclusion:}

	Kernel-level and server-side detection fail on opposite classes of cheat (one blind to hardware-level manipulation, the other blind to a cheat used once and never again), making them complementary layers rather than substitutes \cite{Dorner2024, Collins2024}.
\end{posterbox}
